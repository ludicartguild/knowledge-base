Personal finance from an honest inventory through to financial independence, in the order
the decisions actually have to be made. Each section assumes the previous one is handled,
because doing them out of order is how people end up investing while carrying credit card
debt at 24%.

> [!warning]
> This is educational material, not financial advice. It is written from a US tax and
> account perspective, and account types, limits, and rules differ by country and change
> yearly. Verify current numbers before acting, and talk to a qualified advisor for
> anything involving your specific situation, especially taxes and cross-border matters.

## Objectives

By the end of this path you can:

* Produce an accurate net worth statement and cash flow picture.
* Run a budget you actually follow.
* Choose a debt payoff method with reasons, not by default.
* Size and place an emergency fund correctly.
* Explain the tax-advantaged account types and the order to fill them.
* Build a low-cost index portfolio and state your asset allocation.
* Calculate a retirement number and say what the calculation assumes.
* Evaluate a dividend strategy against total return honestly.

## Prerequisites

Income, expenses, and willingness to look at both. The first section is the hardest one
emotionally and the cheapest one practically.

## How to use this path

Work in order. Each section follows the same rhythm:

* **Focus:** the questions you should be able to answer by the end.
* **Learn:** what to read.
* **Practice:** do it with your actual numbers.
* **Self-check:** you are ready to move on when you can do these.
* **Answer:** collapsed, so you can attempt the self-check first.
* **Ask yourself:** heuristics that test judgement rather than recall.

## 1. Honest inventory

Knowing where you actually are.

**Focus:** What is your net worth, and what is your real monthly cash flow?

**Learn:**
* [Bogleheads wiki: getting started](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Getting_started): the least commercial starting point available.
* [r/personalfinance wiki](https://www.reddit.com/r/personalfinance/wiki/commontopics): the flowchart is genuinely good.

**Practice:** List every asset at current market value and every liability including
informal debts. Subtract. Then list actual income and actual spending for last month from
statements rather than memory.

**Self-check:** you can state your net worth and monthly cash flow from real data.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Assets.** Everything you own with market value: cash, checking, savings, brokerage,
> retirement accounts, home equity, vehicles at market value rather than purchase price,
> and anything else of meaningful worth.
> **Liabilities.** Everything owed: credit cards, student loans, auto loans, mortgage,
> medical debt, personal loans, and money owed to family. Include the informal ones. They
> are the ones people omit and the ones that carry relationship cost as well as financial.
> **Net worth.** Assets minus liabilities. It can be negative, and often is early in a
> working life or after a medical event. A negative number is a starting condition, not a
> verdict, and the only thing it determines is where you begin.
> **Cash flow.** Income minus expenses, from statements rather than recollection. This is
> the number that changes, and it is the one people estimate wrongly in a consistent
> direction.
> **Why this is first.** Every later decision depends on these two numbers. Doing anything
> else first means optimising something you have not measured.

**Ask yourself:**
* Which liability did I hesitate to write down, and why?
* Was my estimate of spending higher or lower than reality, and by how much?

## 2. The budget

The one lever you fully control.

**Focus:** Where does the money actually go? Which budgeting method will you sustain?

**Learn:**
* [YNAB's method](https://www.ynab.com/the-four-rules): zero-based budgeting explained.
* Any category tracker you will actually open. The tool matters less than the habit.

**Practice:** Categorise every transaction for at least thirty days. Then build next
month's budget and follow it. Expect to be wrong in the first month and adjust rather than
abandon it.

**Self-check:** you can name your two largest discretionary categories and explain the
budgeting method you chose.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Track first.** Thirty days of categorised transactions, from statements rather than
> guessing. Most people are surprised, and one or two categories almost always dominate.
> Those two are where the leverage is, and no amount of optimising small categories
> substitutes.
> **50/30/20.** Fifty percent of post-tax income on needs, thirty on wants, twenty on
> savings and debt above minimums. A starting reference point rather than a target.
> Aggressive debt payoff often inverts it to 50/10/40 or sharper, and high cost-of-living
> areas frequently make the fifty impossible.
> **Zero-based budgeting.** Every dollar is assigned a job before the month begins, so
> assignments equal income and no dollar is unaccounted for. Harder than category
> budgeting and meaningfully more effective, because the unassigned dollar is the one that
> disappears.
> **Choosing.** The best method is the one you will still be doing in six months. A
> perfect system abandoned in March loses to a crude one maintained all year.
> **The point.** Income is hard to change quickly and spending is not. The budget is the
> only lever that responds immediately, which is why it comes before everything except
> knowing your numbers.

**Ask yourself:**
* Which category would I be embarrassed to explain, and is that information or shame?
* Am I budgeting the amount I want to spend or the amount I do spend?

## 3. Debt

Getting out, in a defensible order.

**Focus:** Avalanche or snowball? Which debts are genuinely urgent?

**Learn:**
* [Bogleheads: paying down debt](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Paying_down_loans_versus_investing).
* Dave Ramsey's snowball material, read for the behavioural argument rather than the rest.

**Practice:** List every debt with balance, interest rate, and minimum. Pick a method,
write down why, and calculate your payoff date. Then set the payments up automatically.

**Self-check:** you can choose between avalanche and snowball with reasons, and state your
payoff date.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Avalanche.** Minimums on everything, every extra dollar at the highest interest rate,
> then roll that payment into the next. Mathematically optimal: fastest payoff and least
> total interest.
> **Snowball.** Minimums on everything, every extra dollar at the smallest balance, then
> roll forward. Slower and more expensive in interest, and more effective for many people
> because the early wins are visible and keep them in the plan.
> **Which is right.** The mathematically optimal plan you abandon in month four loses to
> the suboptimal one you finish. If you have abandoned payoff plans before, snowball. If
> you have not, avalanche. The difference in total interest is usually smaller than people
> assume unless the balances differ enormously.
> **Hybrid.** Clear any trivial balances first for momentum, then switch to avalanche.
> The most commonly recommended balance between the two.
> **What is actually urgent.** Anything above roughly 8 to 10 percent, and credit card
> debt in particular, should be attacked before investing beyond an employer match,
> because paying off a 24% debt is a guaranteed 24% return and no investment offers that.
> Low-rate debt such as a subsidised student loan or a low mortgage is a different
> question, and paying it early competes directly with investing.

**Ask yourself:**
* Have I abandoned a payoff plan before, and does my chosen method account for that?
* Which of my debts is cheap enough that paying it off early is actually a choice?

## 4. The emergency fund

The thing that stops one bad month becoming debt.

**Focus:** How much, and where does it live?

**Learn:**
* [Bogleheads: emergency fund](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Emergency_fund).

**Practice:** Calculate your essential monthly expenses, which is not your full lifestyle.
Open a high-yield savings account at an insured institution and automate a transfer.

**Self-check:** you can size your fund from essential expenses and explain where it should
and should not be kept.

> [!question]- Answer
> **The starter fund.** Roughly one thousand dollars, built before attacking debt
> aggressively while maintaining minimums. The purpose is behavioural: it absorbs the flat
> tyre and the urgent care visit without sending you back to the credit card and
> undoing months of progress. The maths is arguable; the behaviour is not.
> **The full fund.** Three to six months of essential expenses, built after high-interest
> debt is cleared. Essential means rent, utilities, food, insurance, and minimum payments,
> not your current lifestyle. Lean toward six months if your income is variable, your
> household has one earner, or your field hires slowly.
> **Where it lives.** A high-yield savings account at an insured bank. Not a brokerage,
> because the emergency will arrive during a market drop. Not a certificate of deposit you
> cannot break. Not a checking account paying nothing, which costs you real money for no
> benefit.
> **Why the order matters.** Investing before having a cash buffer means your first
> emergency forces a sale, quite possibly at a loss, quite possibly with a tax
> consequence. The buffer is what lets everything after it stay invested.

**Ask yourself:**
* What are my genuinely essential expenses, stripped of everything optional?
* If I lost my income tomorrow, how many months do I actually have?

## 5. Retirement accounts

The wrappers, and the order to fill them.

**Focus:** What are the account types and what does each do? What is the filling order and
why?

**Learn:**
* [Bogleheads: prioritising investments](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Prioritizing_investments): the canonical order.
* IRS publications for current limits, which change yearly.

**Practice:** Identify every account available to you. Capture the full employer match
today if you are not already. Then write your filling order with reasons.

**Self-check:** you can explain each account type, the traditional against Roth decision,
and why the order is what it is.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Workplace plans.** 401(k), or 403(b), 457(b), or TSP depending on employer.
> Contributions are pre-tax in traditional form or post-tax in Roth. Many employers match
> a percentage, and that match is compensation you forfeit by not contributing.
> **IRA.** A personal account at any brokerage, with an annual limit well below the
> workplace plan's. Roth means paying tax now and withdrawing tax-free; traditional means
> deducting now and paying tax in retirement.
> **HSA.** Available only with a high-deductible health plan, and the most tax-advantaged
> account in the US system: deductible going in, growing untaxed, and withdrawn untaxed
> for medical expenses. After 65 it behaves like a traditional IRA for any purpose. If you
> qualify, it deserves a high priority.
> **Traditional against Roth.** The question is whether your marginal rate is higher now
> or in retirement. Higher now favours traditional; lower now, as early in a career,
> favours Roth. Nobody knows future rates, so splitting across both is a reasonable hedge
> rather than an admission of indecision.
> **The order.** Employer match first, because it is an immediate guaranteed return.
> Then high-interest debt. Then HSA if eligible. Then max the IRA and the workplace plan.
> Then taxable brokerage. The logic is guaranteed returns before probable ones, and tax
> advantage before none.

**Ask yourself:**
* Am I leaving any employer match unclaimed, and what is that costing annually?
* Do I expect to be in a higher or lower bracket in retirement, and on what basis?

## 6. Index investing

What to actually buy.

**Focus:** Why index rather than pick? What is asset allocation and how do you choose one?

**Learn:**
* John Bogle, *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing*.
* [Bogleheads: three-fund portfolio](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Three-fund_portfolio).

**Practice:** Write your target allocation across US stocks, international stocks, and
bonds, with the reasoning. Implement it in one account. Then set a rebalancing rule and
write down what you will do in a 30% drop, before it happens.

**Self-check:** you can explain why index funds win on average, state your allocation with
reasons, and describe your rebalancing rule.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Index against active.** An index fund mechanically tracks a broad market at very low
> cost, often three to ten basis points. Active funds employ people to beat the market and
> charge for it. Over fifteen years and longer, roughly 80 to 90 percent of active funds
> underperform their index after fees, and the minority that outperform are not reliably
> identifiable in advance, which is the part that matters. Past outperformance predicts
> future outperformance poorly.
> **Why cost dominates.** Fees compound against you exactly as returns compound for you.
> One percent annually over thirty years consumes a large fraction of the final balance,
> and it is the only variable in the whole system you control with certainty.
> **The three-fund portfolio.** Total US stock market, total international stock market,
> and total bond market. Three funds give you effectively global diversification at minimal
> cost and require almost no maintenance.
> **Allocation.** The stock-to-bond split is the decision that drives most of your
> volatility. Age-based rules of thumb exist and are crude; the better question is how
> much of a drop you can watch without selling. International allocation is debated between
> roughly 20 and 40 percent of equities, and anywhere in that range is defensible.
> **Rebalancing.** Periodically returning to target, which mechanically sells what has run
> and buys what has lagged. Annually, or on a drift threshold, is plenty. The real function
> is removing the decision from your judgement at exactly the moment your judgement is
> worst.
> **The drop.** Write down now what you will do in a 30 or 40 percent decline. Anyone who
> has not decided in advance decides during, and decides badly.

**Ask yourself:**
* What drop could I watch without selling, honestly, and does my allocation reflect it?
* What am I paying in fees annually, in dollars rather than percent?

## 7. Income

The other side of the equation.

**Focus:** What actually grows income? Why does switching beat staying?

**Learn:**
* [levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi/) or equivalent compensation data for your field.
* Any negotiation material you will actually rehearse. Rehearsal is the active ingredient.

**Practice:** Start a brag document and update it monthly. Find out what your role pays
elsewhere. Then rehearse a negotiation out loud with someone, which is the part people
skip.

**Self-check:** you can state your market rate from data, and you have a current record of
your own accomplishments.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Switching against staying.** Internal raises lag external offers structurally, because
> internal raises are budgeted as a percentage and external offers are priced against the
> market. Switching typically produces 15 to 30 percent; staying typically 3 to 5. Over a
> decade the compounded difference is very large. This is not an argument for constant
> switching, which has its own costs, but it is an argument against assuming loyalty is
> rewarded financially.
> **Negotiating.** Decline the first number. Most people never negotiate at all, and the
> asymmetry is severe: a few uncomfortable minutes against a raise that compounds through
> every future percentage increase and every future offer anchored on it.
> **The brag document.** A running monthly record of what you did and what it produced.
> The single most underrated career tool, because performance reviews and negotiations both
> require evidence and human memory reliably fails to supply it. Writing it monthly takes
> ten minutes; reconstructing a year takes a weekend and produces a worse result.
> **Why this section exists here.** There is a floor on cutting expenses and no ceiling on
> raising income. Once the budget is tight, this is where the remaining leverage is.

**Ask yourself:**
* When did I last find out what my role actually pays elsewhere?
* Could I list my last twelve months of accomplishments right now?

## 8. Tax and protection

Keeping what you have.

**Focus:** What are the real tax levers? What insurance do you actually need?

**Learn:**
* [Bogleheads: tax-efficient fund placement](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Tax-efficient_fund_placement).
* Your own plan documents for disability coverage, which most people have never read.

**Practice:** Check what disability coverage you have through work. Price term life if
anyone depends on your income. Review where your holdings sit across taxable and
tax-advantaged accounts.

**Self-check:** you can name the main tax levers available to you and justify your
insurance coverage.

> [!question]- Answer
> **The biggest tax lever.** Maxing pre-tax retirement contributions, which can reduce
> adjusted gross income substantially. For most people this dwarfs every clever strategy
> discussed elsewhere.
> **Asset location.** Holding tax-inefficient assets such as bonds and REITs inside
> tax-advantaged accounts and tax-efficient broad stock index funds in taxable. Free
> improvement, because it changes nothing about what you own.
> **Tax-loss harvesting.** Selling losers in taxable accounts to offset gains and a limited
> amount of ordinary income. Real but modest, and it requires care around wash-sale rules.
> **Roth conversions.** Moving traditional money to Roth and paying tax now, which is most
> valuable in unusually low-income years such as a career break or early retirement before
> other income starts. Worth an evening with an advisor rather than improvisation.
> **Term life.** Pays out if you die within a fixed term. The right form for nearly
> everyone, cheap while young and healthy, sized at roughly ten times income or enough to
> cover dependents until they are independent and the mortgage is gone. If nobody depends
> on your income, you probably do not need it.
> **Whole and universal life.** Insurance bundled with investment. Expensive, complex,
> illiquid, and frequently mis-sold. Buy term and invest the difference instead. The
> genuine exceptions are estate-planning specific.
> **Disability.** Pays a portion of income if you cannot work. For working-age people
> without dependents it is often more important than life insurance, because you are
> considerably more likely to be disabled during your working years than to die in them.
> Start with employer long-term coverage and supplement if your income depends on a
> specialised skill.

**Ask yourself:**
* Do I know what my disability coverage actually pays, or am I assuming?
* Am I paying for insurance I do not need while missing one I do?

## 9. The long game

Financial independence, and what the number assumes.

**Focus:** How do you calculate a retirement number? What does the 4% rule not account
for?

**Learn:**
* [The Trinity Study](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Trinity_study_update) and its updates.
* [Mr Money Mustache](https://www.mrmoneymustache.com/) for the FIRE framing, read critically.

**Practice:** Calculate your number at 25 times annual expenses. Then recalculate at 33
times, which corresponds to a 3% withdrawal rate. Sit with the difference, because it is
large and it is the honest range.

**Self-check:** you can calculate a retirement number and state the assumptions the
calculation depends on.

> [!question]- Answer
> **The 4% rule.** From the Trinity Study: a retiree withdrawing 4% of the initial
> portfolio annually, inflation-adjusted, had a high probability of lasting 30 years
> across historical conditions. Inverted, your number is roughly 25 times annual expenses.
> **What it assumes and omits.** It assumes a 30-year retirement, so a longer one needs a
> lower rate, commonly 3 to 3.5 percent. It assumes US historical returns, which may not
> repeat. It assumes rigid withdrawals, whereas real retirees cut spending in bad years,
> which helps. It does not address healthcare costs, which in the US are the largest
> uncertainty in the whole calculation. It is a starting estimate, not a guarantee.
> **Sequence of returns risk.** A poor market in the first few years of retirement is far
> more damaging than the same market later, because you are selling into it while the
> portfolio is largest. This is why the early retirement years are the risky ones and why
> flexibility matters most then.
> **FIRE variants.** Lean FIRE means a lower spending target. Fat FIRE means a higher one.
> Barista FIRE means partial income covering the gap. Coast FIRE means having enough
> invested that growth alone reaches your number, so you only need to cover current
> expenses. Coast is the most underrated, because it converts a decades-long grind into a
> single achievable milestone.
> **The uncomfortable arithmetic.** Your savings rate matters far more than your return.
> Saving half your income gets you there in roughly seventeen years regardless of
> reasonable return assumptions, and no allocation cleverness competes with that.

**Ask yourself:**
* What does my number assume about healthcare, and is that assumption defensible?
* Am I closer to Coast FIRE than I think?

## 10. Dividends and going deeper

Two directions people take this, evaluated honestly.

**Focus:** Does a dividend strategy beat total return? What changes when money crosses
borders?

**Learn:**
* [Bogleheads on dividends](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Dividends): the orthodox case.
* Any dividend-growth source, read alongside the orthodox case rather than instead of it.
* For cross-border: a qualified advisor in both jurisdictions. Not a blog.

**Practice:** Compare a dividend-focused fund against a total market fund over twenty
years, on total return after tax. Then, separately, write down what receiving income
rather than selling would do to your behaviour in a downturn.

**Self-check:** you can state the academic case against dividend-focused investing and the
behavioural case for it, and you know what you do not know about cross-border finance.

> [!question]- Answer
> **The orthodox position.** A dollar of dividend and a dollar of capital gain are
> economically identical, since the share price drops by the dividend on the ex-date.
> Dividend-focused investing typically underperforms total market by roughly half a
> percent to one and a half percent annually once tax drag is counted, because dividends
> are taxed when paid whether or not you wanted the income, and because the screen
> concentrates you in particular sectors.
> **The practical counter.** Receiving income rather than selling keeps many investors
> invested through downturns, and staying invested is worth more than the theoretical
> difference. This is a behavioural argument rather than a financial one, and it is a
> legitimate argument, but it should be made honestly as what it is.
> **How to decide.** If you would sell in a crash under a total return approach and would
> not under a dividend approach, the dividend approach is better for you despite being
> worse on paper. If you would hold either way, take the total return.
> **Dividend Aristocrats and Kings.** Companies with 25 and 50 consecutive years of
> increases. A reasonable quality screen and not a guarantee, since a company can and does
> cut after decades of increases.
> **Cross-border.** Genuinely complex and genuinely dangerous to improvise. Residency,
> visa, and citizenship are different things with different tax consequences. Foreign
> property ownership rules vary enormously and sometimes exclude foreigners outright. Tax
> treaties determine whether you are taxed twice. US citizens are taxed on worldwide income
> regardless of residence, and reporting requirements for foreign accounts carry severe
> penalties. Get advice in both jurisdictions before moving money, not after.

**Ask yourself:**
* Would I actually sell in a crash, and does my strategy account for the honest answer?
* What would I be improvising if I moved money across a border?

## Capstone

A written financial plan: your numbers, your budget method, your payoff order and date,
your emergency fund target and location, your account filling order, your allocation with
a rebalancing rule, your retirement number with its assumptions stated, and what you will
do in a 30% drop.

Then automate everything that can be automated, and put a calendar reminder to revisit it
annually.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I know my net worth and real cash flow from actual data.
- [ ] I run a budget I have sustained for more than one month.
- [ ] I chose a payoff method with reasons and know my payoff date.
- [ ] My emergency fund is sized on essential expenses and held somewhere appropriate.
- [ ] I capture my full employer match and know my account filling order.
- [ ] I have a written allocation, a rebalancing rule, and a plan for a crash.
- [ ] I know my market rate and keep a current brag document.
- [ ] I can justify every insurance policy I hold and name any gap.
- [ ] I can calculate my number and state what the calculation assumes.
