The operational reality of running a one-person game studio: the market you are actually
entering, how to pay for it, how to scope a project that can ship, and how to market it
starting long before launch.

This path is deliberately sober. The making of games is covered elsewhere; this is the
business underneath, including the parts that are discouraging. Knowing the numbers before
you commit is worth more than enthusiasm that meets them later.

## Objectives

By the end of this path you can:

* State the actual market conditions rather than the 2015 version of them.
* Choose an income model honestly, including the ones that are not full-time indie.
* Set up an entity, banking, and bookkeeping correctly from day one.
* Build a contracting practice that funds the games.
* Scope a first project that can plausibly ship.
* Run a marketing campaign that starts at announcement rather than at launch.
* Maintain production discipline alone across a multi-year project.
* Decide what to do after the first project, whether it worked or not.

## Prerequisites

The ability to make a game, or a plan to acquire it. This path assumes the craft and
addresses everything around it.

## How to use this path

Sections 1 and 2 are decision points. If they change your mind about doing this, they have
done their job. Each section follows the same rhythm:

* **Focus:** the questions you should be able to answer by the end.
* **Learn:** what to read.
* **Practice:** apply it to your actual situation.
* **Self-check:** you are ready to move on when you can do these.
* **Answer:** collapsed, so you can attempt the self-check first.
* **Ask yourself:** heuristics that test judgement rather than recall.

## 1. The market you are entering

The numbers, before the plan.

**Focus:** How many games ship? What does the median one earn? What does discoverability
actually depend on?

**Learn:**
* [SteamDB](https://steamdb.info/): release volume over time.
* [Chris Zukowski, How To Market A Game](https://howtomarketagame.com/): the most rigorous public data analysis in this space.
* [Game Discoverability Now](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/): Simon Carless on the mechanics.

**Practice:** Look up actual revenue estimates for ten games in your intended genre that
shipped in the last two years, including ones you have not heard of. The ones you have not
heard of are the representative sample.

**Self-check:** you can state the market conditions with numbers and explain what drives
Steam visibility.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Volume.** Steam releases have gone from roughly three thousand games a year in 2014 to
> seven thousand in 2017 to over fourteen thousand in 2024. The denominator of competition
> has roughly quintupled in a decade.
> **Median revenue.** Public estimates put median new-release Steam revenue in the low
> single-digit thousands of dollars. Median is the important word: the distribution is
> extremely skewed, so the average is misleading and the successes you have heard of are
> the tail by definition.
> **The discoverability cliff.** Steam's algorithm is wishlist-driven, and a game
> launching with under about five thousand wishlists is largely invisible. Crossing ten
> thousand takes a sustained campaign over months, not a launch announcement.
> **Press has moved.** Traditional outlets still exist and their distributive weight is
> much smaller than a decade ago. Streamer and influencer coverage matters more, and it
> follows playable demos rather than press releases.
> **What this means.** Marketing is not a phase after development; it is a parallel track
> that starts at announcement. A finished game with no wishlists is in a worse position
> than an unfinished one with an audience. This is the single most common structural
> mistake, and it is not recoverable at launch.
> **Why start here.** Not to discourage, but because every later decision, scope, funding,
> timeline, depends on these numbers being real to you rather than abstract.

**Ask yourself:**
* Have I looked at the games in my genre I have never heard of, or only the successes?
* Do my plans assume the 2015 market or the current one?

## 2. Should you do this?

The decision, taken seriously.

**Focus:** What are the actual income models? Which one are you choosing?

**Learn:** Developer postmortems, especially the ones about games that did not sell. Those
are more informative and much rarer.

**Practice:** Write down your runway in months, your minimum acceptable income, and what
happens if the first project earns nothing. If you cannot answer the third, you have not
made the decision yet.

**Self-check:** you can name the income models and say which you are choosing and why.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Contracting full-time, games on the side.** The most stable path. Income is
> predictable and the game carries no business pressure. Worth stating plainly: many
> people you think of as indie developers are full-time contractors making games in their
> own time.
> **Industry employment, indie on the side.** A studio day job with a project at night.
> Slower and lowest risk, with the advantage that the day job builds relevant skill.
> **Small team with co-founders.** Multiplies capability and reduces the psychological
> load of being alone. Adds equity splits and decision-making friction.
> **Solo with publisher funding.** A salary or advance while building, in exchange for
> creative control and back-end royalties. De-risks living costs and changes whose game it
> is.
> **Full-time solo self-funded.** Maximum control, maximum risk, and the one the culture
> romanticises. It requires runway, and the honest version of the plan states how many
> months of it you have.
> **The reframe worth making.** "Am I doing this full-time" is a different question from
> "am I making games". The part-time paths are not failed versions of the full-time one,
> and treating them that way has pushed a lot of people into unnecessary financial risk.

**Ask yourself:**
* How many months of runway do I have, and what happens at month zero?
* Am I choosing full-time because it fits, or because it sounds more real?

## 3. Studio formation

The administrative base.

**Focus:** Which entity, and what has to be in place before money moves?

**Learn:**
* Your jurisdiction's small business formation guidance.
* [Mercury](https://mercury.com/) or similar business banking aimed at small companies.

**Practice:** Form the entity, open a separate business account, and set up bookkeeping.
Then reconcile it at the end of your first month so the habit exists before the volume
does.

**Self-check:** you can choose an entity for an indie studio and explain why separating
finances is non-negotiable.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Entity.** Sole proprietorship is the default and the worst for liability, because
> there is no separation between you and the business. An LLC is the common indie default:
> flexible, pass-through taxed, and it protects personal assets. An S-Corp election can
> reduce self-employment tax once profit is meaningful. A C-Corp is for raising venture
> capital, which is rare and usually wrong for indie games.
> **Where to form.** Form in your state or country of residence unless you have a
> specific reason otherwise. Forming in Delaware because startups do is a common mistake
> that adds foreign registration and annual filings for no benefit.
> **Banking.** A separate business account, always. Never mix personal and business funds.
> Commingling is what pierces the liability protection you formed the entity for, and it
> turns tax preparation into archaeology.
> **Bookkeeping from day one.** Track income, expenses, and any home-office or equipment
> apportionment, and reconcile monthly. Ten minutes a month beats a week in April, and the
> records are what make deductions defensible.
> **Why this comes before the fun part.** Every one of these is cheap to do at the start
> and expensive to retrofit. Reconstructing two years of mixed transactions is a genuinely
> awful week, and it always happens at the worst time.

**Ask yourself:**
* Is any business expense currently on a personal card?
* Could I produce clean books for last year if asked tomorrow?

## 4. Contracting as the income engine

Paying for the games.

**Focus:** What makes you hireable? What has to be in the contract?

**Learn:**
* Game industry rate discussions in developer communities, which are more candid than
  published surveys.
* Standard contractor agreement templates, reviewed by a lawyer once.

**Practice:** Write your one-line specialisation. Then take one contract and make sure it
specifies scope, payment schedule, revision limits, kill fee, and IP terms.

**Self-check:** you can state a specialisation that gets you hired and name what a contract
must contain.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Specialise.** "I can do anything" reads as hobbyist. Narrow positioning gets hired:
> combat designer, FMOD audio implementer, technical level designer, Unity-to-console
> porter, UI artist. The narrower it is, the easier it is for someone to know they need
> you. Generalists compete on price; specialists compete on fit.
> **Where the work is.** Other indie studios most commonly, AAA studios for short-term
> contracts, publishers, asset stores where you sell tools rather than time, and
> professional communities. Most contracts come through people who already know your work,
> which makes visibility part of the income strategy.
> **Hourly against project rate.** Hourly protects you when scope is unclear. Project rate
> pays better when you are fast and the scope is genuinely bounded. Project rate on unclear
> scope is how people end up working for a fraction of their rate.
> **The contract is the work.** Always written, always before starting, always including
> friends. It must specify scope, payment schedule with a deposit, what counts as a
> revision and how many are included, a kill fee if the client cancels, and who owns what.
> The friction of writing it is a small multiple of nothing compared to the friction of a
> dispute without one.
> **Deposits.** Take one up front, commonly a third. It filters clients who were never
> going to pay and it means a cancellation does not cost you the whole engagement.
> **The trap.** Contracting pays now and the game pays maybe. It is very easy for
> contracting to consume all the time. Ring-fence game time explicitly or it will not
> survive a busy quarter.

**Ask yourself:**
* What is my one-line specialisation, and would someone know from it whether to hire me?
* Did contracting eat my game time last month?

## 5. Funding the project

Beyond your own savings.

**Focus:** What funding is available and what does each cost?

**Learn:**
* Publisher deal terms, discussed publicly by developers who have signed them.
* Regional and platform funds, which vary enormously by country.

**Practice:** Map every funding source available to you with the amount, what they want,
and what you give up. Then decide for this project and write the reasoning down.

**Self-check:** you can map the sources and choose one with the tradeoffs stated.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Self-funded.** Savings plus contracting income. Most indie studios. Maximum creative
> control, minimum capital, longest timeline. The timeline is the real cost and the one
> people underestimate.
> **Publisher funding.** An advance against royalties, often with marketing and porting
> support. You give up a revenue share and sometimes rights. Read the rights reversion
> clause carefully, because that determines whether you still own your game in five years.
> Also read what marketing support actually commits them to, which is frequently less than
> the conversation implied.
> **Grants.** Non-dilutive money from regional arts bodies, national funds, and platform
> holders. Underused because the applications are tedious. Free money is worth the
> paperwork, and grant writing is a reusable skill.
> **Crowdfunding.** Money, and more importantly proof of demand and a mailing list. It is a
> marketing campaign that happens to raise funds, and running it as though it were only
> fundraising is why most campaigns fail. It also needs an existing audience to succeed,
> which means it is not a substitute for section 6.
> **Platform and storefront deals.** Timed exclusivity or featuring in exchange for money
> or reach. Worth evaluating on the actual numbers rather than the prestige.
> **The general rule.** Money that arrives earlier costs more. Decide what you are
> actually buying with the dilution or the revenue share, and whether it is time, reach,
> or reduced risk.

**Ask yourself:**
* What am I actually buying with this funding, and could I buy it another way?
* Do I still own my game in five years under these terms?

## 6. Scoping the first project

Making something that can ship.

**Focus:** What scope can one person finish? What makes a concept discoverable?

**Learn:**
* [Chris Zukowski on genre and discoverability](https://howtomarketagame.com/): which genres have audiences.
* Postmortems from solo developers, particularly on what they cut.

**Practice:** Write your game in 25 words. If you cannot, the concept is not focused
enough. Then name the specific audience as "people who liked X and wished Y were
different", and estimate the timeline. Then double it.

**Self-check:** you can pitch the game in 25 words, name a specific audience, and defend
the scope.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Small scope, big idea.** A clearly differentiated concept achievable in six to
> twenty-four months of full-time work, which becomes twelve to forty-eight months if you
> are contracting alongside. The differentiation matters more than the size; a small
> game that is obviously unlike anything else is more findable than a large generic one.
> **The 25-word pitch.** If you cannot state it in 25 words, the game is not focused
> enough, and that lack of focus will show up later as scope creep and as a trailer nobody
> understands. This is a design tool, not a marketing exercise.
> **A specific audience.** Not "gamers". "People who liked X and wished Y were different"
> is the useful form: genre, plus reference points, plus your delta. This sentence tells
> you which streamers to contact, which subreddits matter, and what the capsule art needs
> to communicate.
> **Genre and discoverability.** Some genres have active, searching audiences and some are
> saturated without a corresponding audience. This shifts over time, so check current data
> rather than relying on a list. The structural point is that genre choice is a marketing
> decision as much as a creative one, and pretending otherwise does not exempt you from it.
> **Scope estimation.** Estimate, then double it. This is not a joke about optimism; it is
> the consistent empirical finding, and the doubling covers the polish, the porting, the
> store page, and the parts that are not making the game.

**Ask yourself:**
* Can I say what my game is in one sentence a stranger would understand?
* What is my delta, specifically, against the closest existing game?

## 7. Marketing from day one

The track that runs parallel to development.

**Focus:** What are the highest-leverage assets? What wishlist numbers matter?

**Learn:**
* [How To Market A Game](https://howtomarketagame.com/): the practical playbook.
* [Game Discoverability Now](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/).

**Practice:** Put a Steam page up as soon as you have a name, a logo, three screenshots,
and a trailer concept. Then track wishlist growth weekly and treat the rate as a signal
rather than the total.

**Self-check:** you can state the wishlist thresholds and name the highest-leverage assets.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Steam page early.** As soon as you have the minimum assets. The page is the wishlist
> surface, and wishlists accumulate over the whole development period. A page up eighteen
> months before launch is worth vastly more than a polished one up at launch.
> **Capsule art is the highest-leverage asset.** It appears as a thumbnail everywhere: in
> search, in recommendations, on streamers' screens. It has to read at very small sizes,
> which is a specific skill. Hire a professional if you cannot make it work at thumbnail
> size, because this single image gates whether anyone clicks at all.
> **The trailer beats screenshots.** People watch trailers and skim screenshots. A 30 to
> 60 second hook trailer, front-loaded with the most distinctive thing about your game, is
> the most-shared asset you will make. The first five seconds decide whether the rest is
> seen.
> **Wishlist thresholds.** Roughly five thousand at launch for basic visibility, ten
> thousand for meaningful launch performance, twenty-five thousand for breakout potential.
> Growth rate matters as much as total: steady accumulation signals sustained interest to
> the algorithm, while a single spike from one viral moment does not.
> **Demos and festivals.** Next Fest and similar events are among the highest-leverage
> wishlist drivers available, and they reward having a demo ready at the right moment
> rather than the best possible demo eventually.
> **The structural point.** Marketing volume now is wishlists later, and wishlists later
> are sales at launch. There is no way to compress this into the final month, which is
> when most solo developers try.

**Ask yourself:**
* Is my Steam page up, and if not what exactly am I waiting for?
* Does my capsule art read at thumbnail size, honestly?

## 8. Production discipline

Sustaining a multi-year project alone.

**Focus:** What routines survive a long project? How do you cut scope on schedule rather
than in crisis?

**Learn:** Solo developer postmortems, read specifically for what they say about the
middle of the project rather than the launch.

**Practice:** Set a daily minimum session and hold it for a month, including on bad days.
Book a monthly scope cut and a quarterly external review in the calendar now.

**Self-check:** you have sustainable routines and a scheduled cutting discipline.

> [!question]- Answer
> **The daily minimum.** A short mandatory session, even on bad days, outperforms heroic
> sprints across months. The purpose is not the output of the two hours; it is that the
> project stays loaded in your head, so you never pay the cost of restarting from cold.
> **Weekly check-in.** Friday: what shipped, what did not, what is blocking, and what
> changes next week. Written down. Without it, weeks blur and you cannot tell a slow
> stretch from a stalled one until months have passed.
> **Monthly cuts.** Once a month, look at scope and cut something. The default direction
> is always adding, and the discipline is subtracting on a schedule rather than in a panic
> at month twenty. Cutting is shipping.
> **Quarterly external review.** Show it to someone outside the project every ninety days.
> This is the only reliable correction for tunnel vision, which is the specific
> occupational hazard of working alone and which you cannot detect from inside.
> **The long middle.** The hardest part is not the start or the launch, it is month nine
> when novelty is gone and the end is not visible. Every routine here exists for that
> stretch, and they have to be in place before it arrives because you will not build them
> during it.
> **Isolation.** Working alone for years is a real risk to judgement and to health. Peers,
> communities, and people who will tell you the truth are infrastructure. Schedule contact
> deliberately; it does not happen by itself when you have no colleagues.

**Ask yourself:**
* What did I cut last month? If nothing, is scope really stable or am I avoiding it?
* Who has seen this game recently who is not me?

## 9. Launch and after

Shipping, and deciding what is next.

**Focus:** What does a launch actually involve? How do you read an underperforming launch?

**Learn:** Launch-week postmortems, especially from games that sold modestly, which are
more common and more instructive than breakout stories.

**Practice:** Write your launch plan four months out: beats, festival participation,
streamer outreach, press, and the day itself. Then write the postmortem template you will
fill in afterwards, before you know the outcome.

**Self-check:** you can plan a launch as a campaign and describe how you would evaluate the
result.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Launch is a campaign, not a day.** Beats over months: announcement, demo, festival
> participation, a release date announcement, then launch. Each is an opportunity for
> coverage and wishlist growth, and the release date announcement specifically is
> underused.
> **Streamer outreach.** Personal, targeted, and based on whether they actually play your
> genre. Keys to people whose audience would want this. A mass key blast is noise, and it
> burns the list you could have used.
> **Launch week.** Be present and responsive: bugs, reviews, community. The first days
> shape the review score, and the review score shapes everything afterwards through the
> algorithm.
> **After launch.** The long tail is real. Sales events, updates, and ports continue to
> produce revenue for years, and for many modest games the tail exceeds launch week.
> Writing the game off after two weeks is premature.
> **Reading an underperforming launch.** The postmortem is the data, and the useful
> question is where it failed: the game, the marketing, the scope, the genre, or the
> timing. These have different remedies and they are distinguishable. Weak wishlists with
> strong reviews points at marketing. Strong wishlists with weak conversion points at the
> store page or the demo. Both weak points at the concept.
> **The second project decision.** If contracting is paying well, the honest question is
> whether another game is the right next investment or whether the contracting practice has
> itself become the studio. Either is a legitimate outcome, and only one of them is
> discussed at conferences.

**Ask yourself:**
* If this underperforms, which of the five failure points would I check first?
* Would I be happy if the contracting practice turned out to be the business?

## Capstone

A written studio plan: your income model with runway stated, your entity and books set up,
your specialisation for contracting, your project in 25 words with its audience and scope,
your marketing plan from announcement to launch, and your production routines in the
calendar.

Then a Steam page, live, with wishlists accumulating.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state current market conditions with real numbers.
- [ ] I chose an income model deliberately and know my runway.
- [ ] My entity, banking, and bookkeeping are set up and separate.
- [ ] I have a specialisation and a contract template that protects me.
- [ ] I can map funding sources and say what each costs.
- [ ] I can pitch my game in 25 words to a specific audience.
- [ ] My Steam page is up and I track wishlist growth weekly.
- [ ] I have daily, weekly, monthly, and quarterly routines in place.
- [ ] I have a launch plan and a postmortem template written in advance.
