A comparative path through traditions that share one structural claim: that the ordinary
human condition is a kind of entrapment, and that there is a way out. Gnosticism,
Neoplatonism, Western mysticism, Eastern soteriologies, and Hellenistic practical
philosophy, taken on their own terms and then examined critically.

This path describes what traditions claim, not whether they are true. It takes each
seriously enough to state its position as a practitioner would recognise it, then gives a
full section to the strongest critiques. Both halves are the point.

A note on the practice sections: contemplative practice has real effects, and the
traditions themselves warn that some of them are difficult. Section 8 says more about
that.

## Objectives

By the end of this path you can:

* Use the comparative vocabulary: soteriology, theodicy, esoteric and exoteric.
* State the Gnostic cosmology accurately, and distinguish it from its popular caricature.
* Explain the Platonic ascent and the Plotinian hypostases.
* Compare Buddhist, Vedantic, and Taoist accounts of liberation without collapsing them.
* Distinguish philosophy as a way of life from philosophy as an academic discipline.
* Engage the strongest analytical and naturalist critiques on their merits.
* Maintain a contemplative practice and describe its effects honestly.
* Write a working philosophy that says what you have refused and why.

## Prerequisites

None, though comfort with reading difficult primary texts slowly will serve you better
than any background. This is a slow path. It rewards years rather than weeks.

## How to use this path

Work in order. Sections 2 through 7 each present a tradition on its own terms; section 8
is where the critiques come in, and reading it early will keep you from ever taking any
tradition seriously enough to understand it.

Each section follows the same rhythm:

* **Focus:** the questions you should be able to answer by the end.
* **Learn:** the primary texts, and the scholarly treatment.
* **Practice:** reading, writing, or sitting.
* **Self-check:** you are ready to move on when you can do these.
* **Answer:** collapsed, so you can attempt the self-check first.
* **Ask yourself:** heuristics that test understanding rather than recall.

> [!tip]
> Read primary sources before commentary wherever you can bear to. Most disagreements
> about these traditions are disagreements about summaries.

## 1. The question and the vocabulary

What is being asked, and the terms for asking it.

**Focus:** What is soteriology and why compare across traditions? What does the esoteric
and exoteric distinction mark?

**Learn:**
* [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/): reliable entry points for every term here.
* Aldous Huxley, *The Perennial Philosophy*: the framing, worth knowing and worth contesting.

**Practice:** Write your own statement of the question, before reading anyone's answer. A
paragraph on what you think is wrong with the ordinary condition, if anything. Date it and
keep it; section 9 asks you to return to it.

**Self-check:** you can define the comparative vocabulary and explain what the perennialist
claim asserts and why it is contested.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Soteriology.** The doctrine of salvation or liberation within a tradition: what the
> problem is, what the solution is, and what the transformed state looks like. Comparing
> soteriologies is sharper than comparing theologies, because it forces each tradition to
> say what it thinks is actually wrong.
> **Theodicy.** The question of why a good and powerful God permits evil. Worth noting
> that Gnosticism does not answer this question, it dissolves it, by positing that the
> creator of the material world is not the ultimate good. That move is elegant and costly,
> and section 8 returns to whether it is coherent.
> **Esoteric and exoteric.** Most traditions have an outer form for general practice and
> an inner reading for those who go further. The traditions in this path are
> overwhelmingly esoteric, which is worth naming because it shapes their sources: they
> were often written for people already inside a practice.
> **The perennial philosophy.** Huxley's term for the claim that a common mystical core
> underlies the world's traditions. Useful as a framing because it makes comparison
> possible. Contested because it can flatten genuine differences: Buddhist emptiness and
> Vedantic Brahman are not obviously two descriptions of one thing, and treating them as
> such assumes what it should argue.

**Ask yourself:**
* What do I actually think is wrong with the ordinary condition, in my own words?
* Am I looking for a tradition, or for confirmation of something I already hold?

## 2. Gnosticism

The tradition that names the world as the problem.

**Focus:** What is the Gnostic cosmology? What does gnosis mean, and how does it differ
from faith or from knowledge?

**Learn:**
* [The Nag Hammadi Library](http://gnosis.org/naghamm/nhl.html): the primary texts, freely available.
* Elaine Pagels, *The Gnostic Gospels*: the accessible scholarly entry.
* Hans Jonas, *The Gnostic Religion*: the deeper philosophical treatment.

**Practice:** Read the Gospel of Thomas and the Apocryphon of John directly before any
commentary. Note where the second is genuinely strange rather than merely unfamiliar.

**Self-check:** you can state the cosmology accurately, define gnosis, and distinguish
historical Gnosticism from its modern popular version.

> [!question]- Answer
> **The cosmology.** Beyond the material world is the Pleroma, the Fullness, containing
> aeons which are divine emanations. Sophia, divine Wisdom and usually the youngest aeon,
> falls or errs, and the result is the Demiurge, an inferior creator who makes the
> material world and believes himself the only god. Fragments of true divinity, the spark
> or pneuma, are trapped in human beings. Liberation is the awakening of that spark
> through gnosis and its return.
> **Gnosis.** Direct experiential knowing, not propositional belief and not inference. The
> distinction from faith matters: faith trusts a claim, gnosis is presented as recognition.
> That is also precisely what makes it hard to assess from outside, which section 8
> addresses.
> **The Demiurge.** Often identified in Gnostic readings with the God of the Hebrew Bible,
> who is emphatically not the ultimate God in this scheme. This identification is why
> Gnosticism was treated as heretical rather than merely heterodox.
> **Historical against popular.** Historical Gnosticism was a diverse set of movements in
> the first centuries, mostly known through hostile sources until Nag Hammadi was found in
> 1945. The modern popular version tends to flatten this into a single tidy system, and to
> read it as straightforward anti-cosmic pessimism. The actual texts are stranger, more
> varied, and often more ambivalent about the world than the summary suggests.
> **What is genuinely at stake.** If the world is a mistake, the ethical consequences are
> serious and were contested inside the tradition itself: some groups concluded rigorous
> asceticism, others that the material simply did not matter.

**Ask yourself:**
* If the world were a mistake, what would follow about how to live in it?
* Where am I reading these texts as confirming something I already believed?

## 3. Platonism and Neoplatonism

The philosophical architecture underneath much of the rest.

**Focus:** What is the ascent in Plato? What are the three hypostases in Plotinus?

**Learn:**
* Plato, *Republic* Book VII and *Symposium*: the cave and the ascent through eros.
* Plotinus, *Enneads*: difficult, and worth reading in selections with a guide.
* Pierre Hadot, *Plotinus, or the Simplicity of Vision*.

**Practice:** Read the Cave allegory and write what it claims about knowledge, in your own
words, without using the word "shadow". The paraphrase is the test.

**Self-check:** you can explain the Forms, the ascent through eros, and the relation of
the One, Nous, and Soul.

> [!question]- Answer
> **The cave.** Prisoners chained facing a wall take shadows for reality. One is freed,
> turns, and climbs to the sun. The claim is that ordinary perception is not merely
> incomplete but systematically misleading, and that the correction is difficult, gradual,
> and initially painful. The detail people forget is that the freed prisoner returning to
> the cave is not believed.
> **The Forms.** The perceived world is changing and imperfect; behind it are eternal
> unchanging Forms, with the Good highest. Knowledge is recollection of what the soul knew
> before embodiment, which is why learning feels like recognition.
> **Eros as ascent.** In the Symposium, love begins with one beautiful body and ascends
> through bodies, souls, practices, and knowledge to Beauty itself. The structure matters:
> the lower rungs are not rejected, they are the means. This is a significant contrast with
> the Gnostic move.
> **The three hypostases.** The One is beyond being and beyond description, so that even
> calling it one is a concession. From it emanates Nous, Intellect, which contains the
> Forms and thinks itself. From Nous emanates Soul, the principle of individuated life, and
> the cosmos is Soul extended into matter.
> **Why emanation rather than creation.** Nothing is decided or made; the lower proceeds
> from the higher necessarily, as light from a source. This is why Neoplatonism does not
> have a theodicy problem in the same form, and why matter is the far edge of being rather
> than an enemy.

**Ask yourself:**
* Does the Neoplatonic view make the material world a problem, or a distance?
* Where does my own thinking assume something like Forms?

## 4. Western mystical traditions

Hermeticism, Kabbalah, Christian mysticism, Sufism.

**Focus:** What does each add? Where do they converge and where does that convergence get
overstated?

**Learn:**
* Brian Copenhaver, trans., *Hermetica*: the scholarly translation.
* Gershom Scholem, *Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism*.
* Pseudo-Dionysius, *The Mystical Theology*; *The Cloud of Unknowing*.
* Rumi and Ibn Arabi, with a scholarly introduction rather than a loose translation.

**Practice:** Read one primary text from two of these traditions. Write down where they
agree and where the agreement is a translation artifact rather than a real convergence.

**Self-check:** you can characterise each tradition's contribution and identify where
perennialist reading overstates the overlap.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Hermeticism.** A body of Greco-Egyptian texts teaching correspondence between the
> cosmic and the human, "as above, so below", and a path of ascent through knowledge.
> Historically important far beyond its size because of its influence on Renaissance
> thought. Note that the Kybalion is an early twentieth-century popularisation and not an
> ancient source, which is a common and consequential confusion.
> **Kabbalah.** Jewish mysticism, with the sefirot as a structure of divine emanation, and
> concepts including tzimtzum, the divine contraction making room for creation. Scholem's
> scholarship is the reason this is studied historically rather than only practised.
> **Christian mysticism.** The apophatic tradition in particular: Pseudo-Dionysius and The
> Cloud of Unknowing hold that God is approached by negation, since every positive
> statement is more unlike God than like. Meister Eckhart pushes this furthest and was
> condemned for it.
> **Sufism.** Islamic mysticism, with fana, the annihilation of the self, and baqa,
> subsistence in God. Ibn Arabi's metaphysics and Rumi's poetry are doing different things
> and are often conflated in Western reception.
> **Real convergence and overstated convergence.** The genuine structural convergence is
> apophasis: several traditions independently conclude that the ultimate is better
> approached by negation. What is overstated is doctrinal agreement. These traditions
> disagree substantially about whether the self persists, whether the world is good, and
> what the relationship between creator and creation is. Translation makes them sound
> closer than they are, because translators of mystical texts reach for a shared English
> vocabulary that the originals do not share.

**Ask yourself:**
* When two traditions sound identical, am I reading them or their translators?
* Which of these is asking a question I actually have?

## 5. Eastern soteriologies

Buddhism, Vedanta, Taoism.

**Focus:** What is each tradition's diagnosis of the problem? Why does the difference
between emptiness and Brahman matter?

**Learn:**
* Walpola Rahula, *What the Buddha Taught*: the best single-book introduction.
* *The Dhammapada*; the Heart and Diamond Sutras for the Mahayana treatment of emptiness.
* The *Upanishads*; Shankara on Advaita Vedanta.
* The *Tao Te Ching* and the *Zhuangzi*.

**Practice:** Write one page on how Buddhist anatta and Vedantic atman are incompatible,
and then one page on why practitioners of both nonetheless report similar experiences. Hold
both.

**Self-check:** you can state each tradition's diagnosis and cure, and explain why
emptiness and Brahman are not two words for one thing.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Buddhism.** The diagnosis is dukkha, unsatisfactoriness, arising from craving, which
> arises from ignorance of how things actually are: impermanent, unsatisfactory, and
> without fixed self. The cure is the eightfold path. Mahayana adds emptiness, shunyata,
> the claim that nothing possesses inherent independent existence, and the bodhisattva
> ideal of delaying one's own liberation.
> **Vedanta.** Advaita Vedanta holds that atman, the self, is identical with Brahman, the
> absolute, and that the appearance of separateness is maya. Liberation is recognition of
> an identity that was always the case.
> **Why they are not the same.** Buddhism denies a permanent self, anatta. Advaita asserts
> one and identifies it with the absolute. These are contradictory claims about what is
> ultimately the case, and the traditions argued about it for centuries, in detail, aware
> of each other. Perennialist readings that treat them as one insight in two vocabularies
> are discarding the argument rather than resolving it.
> **Why practitioners nonetheless report similarly.** A serious possibility is that
> contemplative practice produces a recognisable class of experience, and that traditions
> interpret it through their own doctrine. That is compatible with the experiences being
> real and with the doctrines genuinely conflicting. It is also, as section 8 notes,
> exactly the constructivist argument against treating the experience as evidence.
> **Taoism.** A different shape: the Tao is the way things are, and wu wei, effortless
> action, is alignment rather than attainment. Less a path out than a path of ceasing to
> struggle against, which is why it sits oddly in a comparison built around escape.

**Ask yourself:**
* Am I comfortable holding two traditions I find compelling that contradict each other?
* Which diagnosis of the problem actually matches my experience?

## 6. Hellenistic practical philosophy

Philosophy as a way of life rather than a body of doctrine.

**Focus:** What does Stoicism actually prescribe? How does it differ from the modern
popular version? What did Epicureanism really claim?

**Learn:**
* Epictetus, *Enchiridion*, first. Then the *Discourses*.
* Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*; Seneca, *Letters from a Stoic*.
* Pierre Hadot, *Philosophy as a Way of Life* and *The Inner Citadel*.

**Practice:** Run one Stoic practice daily for a month: the morning premeditation, the
evening review, or the dichotomy of control applied to one recurring frustration. Keep a
log of what changed and what did not.

**Self-check:** you can state the dichotomy of control, explain how Hadot reframes ancient
philosophy, and correct the popular misreading of both Stoicism and Epicureanism.

> [!question]- Answer
> **The dichotomy of control.** Some things are up to us, our judgements, intentions, and
> responses, and some are not, including our bodies, reputations, and everything external.
> Distress comes from treating the second category as the first. The practice is
> continuous reassignment, and it is harder than it sounds because the boundary moves under
> examination.
> **Hadot's reframing.** Ancient philosophy was a set of spiritual exercises aimed at
> transforming how one lives, not a body of propositions to be assessed. Reading Marcus
> Aurelius as a systematic treatise misses that it is a private journal of someone
> practising. This reframing is why Hellenistic philosophy belongs on this path at all: it
> shares the structure of diagnosis, practice, and transformed state.
> **The popular misreading of Stoicism.** Modern popular Stoicism tends toward emotional
> suppression and individual optimisation. Actual Stoicism holds that virtue is the only
> good, that the cosmos is rationally ordered, and that one has duties to the human
> community as a citizen of the world. Stripping the metaphysics and the politics leaves a
> productivity technique, which is not the same thing and is considerably easier to sell.
> **Epicureanism.** Not hedonism in the popular sense. Pleasure is defined as the absence
> of bodily pain and mental disturbance, ataraxia, and the prescription is a modest life
> among friends, with the study of nature used to remove the fear of death and the gods.
> Closer to the Stoics in practice than the caricature suggests.
> **Scepticism.** The Pyrrhonist claim that suspending judgement itself produces
> tranquillity. Worth taking seriously as a rival to every other section here, since it
> proposes that the way out is to stop asking.

**Ask yourself:**
* Which of my current frustrations concerns something not up to me?
* Am I attracted to the metaphysics, or to the technique with the metaphysics removed?

## 7. Contemplative practice

Doing it rather than reading about it.

**Focus:** What do the major practice traditions actually ask you to do? What should you
know before starting?

**Learn:**
* Shunryu Suzuki, *Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind*; a Vipassana manual such as Mahasi or Goenka.
* Martin Laird, *Into the Silent Land*: Christian contemplative practice.
* Willoughby Britton's [Cheetah House](https://www.cheetahhouse.org/) research on adverse effects.

**Practice:** Choose one practice and one tradition and hold it for at least three months
without switching. Sit daily, briefly at first. Keep a log of what happens, including the
unremarkable days, which will be most of them.

**Self-check:** you have sustained a practice, you can describe its effects honestly, and
you know what the traditions warn about.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Vipassana.** Insight practice: sustained mindful attention to breath, body,
> sensations, and mind, with the aim of directly seeing impermanence and the absence of a
> fixed self rather than believing it.
> **Zen.** Zazen, sitting itself, in Soto; koan work in Rinzai. Notably resistant to
> explaining what it is for, which is part of the method rather than an oversight.
> **Dzogchen and Mahamudra.** Pointing-out instruction toward rigpa, pristine awareness,
> traditionally requiring a teacher because the instruction is transmitted rather than
> read.
> **Christian contemplative prayer.** Centering prayer and the apophatic tradition
> revived, in which a word is used to release thought rather than to focus it.
> **Why one tradition at a time.** The instructions differ in ways that conflict.
> Switching between them tends to produce a practice that is none of them, and the
> traditions are close to unanimous that depth comes from duration rather than variety.
> **What the traditions warn about, and the research confirms.** Intensive practice can
> produce difficult states: anxiety, depersonalisation, disrupted sleep, and destabilising
> experiences. The traditional literatures have names for these stages. Contemporary
> research, particularly Britton's, documents them in secular settings where teachers were
> not prepared for them. This is not a reason to avoid practice, but it is a reason to go
> gradually, to keep a teacher or community, and to take a difficult period seriously
> rather than pushing through it. Anyone with a history of trauma or psychosis should talk
> to someone qualified before undertaking intensive retreat practice.

**Ask yourself:**
* Am I practising, or reading about practice and counting it?
* If a difficult period arrived, who would I talk to?

## 8. Critique and scepticism

The strongest opposition, engaged properly.

**Focus:** What is the constructivist argument against mystical experience as evidence?
What is the political critique of gnostic thinking? What does the naturalist account
explain?

**Learn:**
* Steven Katz, on the cultural conditioning of mystical experience; against William James and W.T. Stace.
* Eric Voegelin on gnosticism in modern political movements.
* Daniel Dennett, *Breaking the Spell*; Sam Harris, *Waking Up*.

**Practice:** Write the strongest possible case against the tradition you find most
compelling. Not a fair summary, the strongest case. If you cannot make it hurt, you do not
yet understand the objection.

**Self-check:** you can state each critique in its strongest form and say what it does and
does not establish.

> [!question]- Answer
> **The constructivist critique.** James and Stace treat mystical experience as evidence
> for something beyond the subject. Katz and Proudfoot argue that mystical experience is
> always culturally conditioned: the Christian mystic has Christian experiences, the
> Buddhist has Buddhist ones, and there is no unmediated experience to appeal to. This is
> the hardest objection on the list, because it explains the cross-traditional similarity
> the perennialists point at, and because it does not require denying that the experiences
> occur. What it establishes is that the experience cannot by itself settle which
> tradition is right. What it does not establish is that the experiences are worthless or
> that no tradition is right.
> **The political critique.** Voegelin argued that modern totalitarian movements share a
> structure with gnosticism: a secret knowledge held by an elect, a corrupt world to be
> escaped or destroyed, and immanentising the end of history. The critique bites because
> the structure really is recurrent. It is contested as a reading of actual historical
> Gnosticism, which was mostly quietist rather than revolutionary, and it is better taken
> as a warning about a pattern than as history.
> **The naturalist account.** Religion as a natural phenomenon with cognitive and
> evolutionary explanations. Harris's position is interesting because he accepts the value
> of contemplative practice while rejecting the metaphysics, which makes it a sharper
> challenge than straightforward dismissal: it says you can have section 7 without sections
> 2 through 6.
> **Buddhism's own self-critique.** Worth noting that the tradition contains its own
> sceptical resources, including the warning against attachment to attainments and to
> views, which applies to Buddhist views.
> **What honest engagement requires.** If you can only state the objections in a form
> their proponents would reject, you are rehearsing rather than engaging.

**Ask yourself:**
* Which objection do I keep deflecting rather than answering?
* If the constructivist argument is right, what in my position survives?

## 9. Your own working philosophy

The deliverable.

**Focus:** What do you actually hold, what have you refused, and what remains open?

**Learn:** Reread your paragraph from section 1 and your notes from every section since.
The refusals are the most informative part.

**Practice:** Write your working philosophy. What you take to be the case, what practice
follows from it, what you have refused and why, and what you are leaving genuinely
unresolved. Date it. Plan to revise it in a year.

**Self-check:** you have a written position, it names refusals and open questions, and it
has changed something about how you live.

> [!question]- Answer
> There is no correct answer here. What distinguishes a useful document from a decorative
> one:
>
> **It names refusals specifically.** "I do not accept the anti-cosmic conclusion, because
> the Neoplatonic account of matter as distance rather than error explains the same data
> without it" is a position. Silently omitting Gnosticism is not.
> **It leaves things open honestly.** A working philosophy with no unresolved questions is
> not finished, it is unexamined. Naming what you cannot currently settle is a stronger
> position than a tidy system.
> **It has a practice attached.** Every tradition on this path holds that understanding
> without practice is incomplete. A document that changes nothing you do is a description
> of your reading.
> **It survives the strongest objection.** If section 8's best argument would demolish
> your position and you have not addressed it, you have written a preference.
> **Service appears.** Nearly every tradition here arrives independently at the claim that
> practice which does not issue in service to others is incomplete: works of mercy,
> bodhisattva activity, Sufi service, Stoic citizenship. The shape differs and the
> requirement recurs, which is at minimum worth explaining.
>
> **On teachers and community.** Most of these traditions hold that a living teacher
> becomes necessary at some stage and that the path is harder alone. Whether you accept
> that claim is part of what your document should say.

**Ask yourself:**
* What has changed about how I live, as opposed to what I know?
* Which question did I leave out because I could not answer it?

## Capstone

A written working philosophy, dated, naming what you hold, what you refuse, what remains
open, and what you practise. Then a sustained practice in one tradition for a year, with
the document revised at the end against what the year actually taught you.

The revision is the real artefact. The first version is mostly a record of what you read.
