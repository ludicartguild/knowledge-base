The strategic, legal, and financial side of building a company alone. It starts by asking
whether "startup" is even the right frame, because for most of the people who read this
kind of material it is not, and adopting the wrong frame costs years.

Written with game studios as the running example, but the structure applies to any solo
founder deciding between a business that pays them and a business that scales.

> [!warning]
> Legal and tax structures are jurisdiction-specific and change. This is orientation, not
> legal or financial advice. Every entity, equity, and contract decision here deserves an
> actual lawyer once, and once is usually enough.

## Objectives

By the end of this path you can:

* Distinguish a small business, a startup, and a studio, and say which you are building.
* Apply Lean Startup and Customer Development where they fit and name where they do not.
* Decide deliberately between co-founder, solo, and a strong second.
* Choose an entity structure that matches your funding intent.
* Map the funding landscape and pick a path per project.
* Assemble traction, story, and team into something an investor engages with.
* Read a SAFE and understand what a cap table does over successive rounds.
* Contract with people without losing your IP.

## Prerequisites

Something you want to build, and enough financial runway to think clearly. Section 1 is
where most of the value is, so do not skip it because you have already decided.

## How to use this path

Work in order. Each section follows the same rhythm:

* **Focus:** the questions you should be able to answer by the end.
* **Learn:** what to read.
* **Practice:** apply it to what you are actually building.
* **Self-check:** you are ready to move on when you can do these.
* **Answer:** collapsed, so you can attempt the self-check first.
* **Ask yourself:** heuristics that test judgement rather than recall.

## 1. Is "startup" the right frame?

The decision that determines everything downstream.

**Focus:** What separates a startup from a small business from a studio? Which are you
actually building?

**Learn:**
* Paul Graham, [Startup = Growth](http://www.paulgraham.com/growth.html): the definition worth arguing with.
* [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/): the alternative model, with people living in it.

**Practice:** Write one paragraph describing the business you want in five years. Then
categorise it honestly. If the honest answer is a small business, most of the rest of this
path becomes optional and section 4 changes completely.

**Self-check:** you can distinguish the three models and say which you are building, with
reasons.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Small or lifestyle business.** Ships products, pays the founder a salary, perhaps
> employs contractors. Does not scale fast and does not need to. Most successful indie
> game studios are this. Usually an LLC, usually should not raise venture capital.
> **Startup proper.** A scalable, defensible business that venture capital will fund,
> which for game makers means live service with recurring revenue, a platform or tools
> company adjacent to games, free-to-play with a real economy, or a studio with a roadmap
> of related IP forming a category. The large majority of single-project indie studios are
> not this, and calling yourself one does not make the economics work.
> **Studio or production company.** Closer to a film studio: per-project funding through
> publisher deals or crowdfunding, with equity in the IP rather than in a recurring
> business. Many successful indie studios are this hybrid, and it is the least discussed
> of the three.
> **Why the frame matters so much.** Venture capital needs a return profile that most
> businesses cannot produce. Taking it commits you to attempting a scale you may not want,
> with a board that will hold you to it. A profitable business that pays you well is a
> failure by venture standards and a success by most other measures. Choosing the wrong
> frame does not just misallocate effort, it hands someone else the definition of your
> success.
> **The honest test.** If your plan has no plausible path to a very large outcome, you
> are not a startup in the funding sense, and that is fine. It only becomes a problem if
> you raise as though you were.

**Ask yourself:**
* Do I want to run this in ten years, or sell it?
* Whose definition of success am I about to adopt?

## 2. Startup methodology

The canonical frameworks, and their limits for creative products.

**Focus:** What do Lean Startup and Customer Development actually prescribe? Where does
the model fit badly?

**Learn:**
* Steve Blank, *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*: read before Ries, since Ries built on it.
* Eric Ries, *The Lean Startup*.

**Practice:** Take your riskiest assumption and design the cheapest experiment that would
falsify it. Then run it. The design is the exercise; most people design experiments that
cannot fail.

**Self-check:** you can run a build-measure-learn loop and explain where the model fits
games badly.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Lean Startup.** Build, measure, learn, with validated learning as the unit of progress
> and an explicit pivot-or-persevere decision. The genuine contribution is treating each
> assumption as falsifiable and testing the riskiest first.
> **Customer Development.** Get out of the building. Search for problem-solution fit
> before product-market fit, through discovery, validation, creation, and company
> building. Slower and more rigorous than Lean Startup, and better where the problem
> itself is uncertain.
> **Where it fits games badly.** Games are closer to film than to SaaS. There is no
> problem being solved that a customer can articulate, so customer discovery interviews
> produce little. A minimum viable game is often not fun, and not-fun tells you nothing
> about whether the finished thing would be. Players cannot tell you what they want from a
> novel experience, and the most successful games routinely test poorly in fragments.
> **What still transfers.** Test the riskiest assumption first. Prototype before
> committing. Distinguish what you know from what you believe. For a game the risky
> assumption is usually "is the core loop fun", and the cheap test is a prototype in front
> of strangers rather than an interview.
> **The failure mode.** Applying the framework literally to a creative product produces
> committee-designed work optimised against feedback from people who have not seen the
> whole. Knowing where a methodology does not apply is part of using it.

**Ask yourself:**
* What is my riskiest assumption, and have I designed a test that could actually fail?
* Am I applying a SaaS framework to something that is not SaaS?

## 3. Solo founder reality

The structural disadvantages, named.

**Focus:** Should you find a co-founder? What compensates if you do not?

**Learn:**
* [Y Combinator on co-founders](https://www.ycombinator.com/library): their position, which is strongly pro-co-founder and worth understanding as a position.
* Founder mental health resources. Take this section seriously.

**Practice:** Write down the three functions you are worst at. For each, decide whether you
will learn it, hire it, or accept it. Then build your advisor bench for the ones you
accepted.

**Self-check:** you can make the co-founder decision deliberately and describe what
compensates for solo structural weaknesses.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Find a co-founder if.** The work spans complementary skills you genuinely lack, you do
> not want to be alone with a decade of decisions, or you already have a strong candidate.
> The bar is high because a bad co-founder is worse than none and much harder to remove.
> **Stay solo if.** You have considered candidates seriously and they are weak, the work
> is genuinely within one person's range, or you have a network and advisors that
> compensate. Solo is a real choice and not a consolation.
> **The strong second.** A senior operations lead or fractional COO for founders who want
> leverage without a co-founder. They receive meaningful employee equity rather than
> founder equity, typically low single digits.
> **What investors think.** Many institutional investors are structurally sceptical of
> solo founders, on the grounds that there is nobody to catch mistakes and a single point
> of failure. This is a real fundraising headwind, and the counter is an unusually strong
> advisor bench and unusually strong traction. Knowing the objection lets you answer it
> rather than be surprised.
> **The part that gets skipped.** Solo founding is isolating in a way that compounds. No
> one else feels the weight, no one else can validate a decision, and the failure modes
> are burnout and distorted judgement rather than any business problem. Building a support
> structure, peers, a therapist, founder communities, is infrastructure rather than
> indulgence, and building it before the crisis is the whole point.

**Ask yourself:**
* Who tells me I am wrong, and do I listen to them?
* What would happen to this company if I were unable to work for two months?

## 4. Legal structure and the cap table

Decisions that are expensive to reverse.

**Focus:** Which entity fits your funding intent? What does vesting protect?

**Learn:**
* [Clerky](https://www.clerky.com/) or [Stripe Atlas](https://stripe.com/atlas) documentation for standard formation.
* [Holloway Guide to Equity Compensation](https://www.holloway.com/g/equity-compensation).

**Practice:** Decide your entity with reasons tied to section 1. Model your cap table
through a friends and family round and a seed round, so you can see the dilution before it
happens rather than after.

**Self-check:** you can choose an entity for your funding path and explain vesting and
dilution.

> [!question]- Answer
> **C-Corp, usually Delaware.** The standard for venture-track companies and effectively
> required for institutional funding. Permits preferred stock, clean cap tables, an option
> pool, and an eventual public listing. Costs more to maintain and taxes at the entity
> level.
> **LLC.** The standard for small businesses and studios. Pass-through taxation, which is
> simpler in low-revenue years, and flexible structure. Institutional investors generally
> will not fund an LLC, which is the constraint that matters. Converting later is possible
> and has tax consequences.
> **S-Corp.** A tax election rather than an entity type, sometimes useful for a profitable
> bootstrapped LLC to reduce self-employment tax.
> **Vesting, including your own.** Founder shares should vest, typically over four years
> with a one-year cliff, even when you are the only founder. It protects the company if a
> co-founder leaves early, and investors will require it anyway. A solo founder skipping
> it creates a problem at the first raise.
> **Dilution.** Every round issues new shares, so your percentage falls. This is normal
> and the relevant question is whether the smaller share of a larger company is worth
> more. Model it before you raise, because founders who have not are repeatedly surprised
> by where they land after a seed and a Series A.
> **The option pool.** Investors typically require it be created before their investment,
> which means it dilutes you rather than them. This is standard, negotiable at the margin,
> and worth understanding before the term sheet rather than during.

**Ask yourself:**
* Does my entity match the funding path I actually intend, or the one I might want?
* Have I modelled where I land after two rounds?

## 5. The funding landscape

What money is available and what each kind costs.

**Focus:** What are the sources and what does each want? What does venture money commit
you to?

**Learn:**
* [Y Combinator's SAFE documents](https://www.ycombinator.com/documents): read the actual instrument.
* Game-specific: publisher deal structures, platform funds, and regional grants, which vary by country.

**Practice:** Map every funding source plausibly available to you with the amount, what
they want, and what they cost. Then pick one for your current project and write why the
others are wrong for it.

**Self-check:** you can map the sources and choose a path per project with reasons.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Bootstrap.** Founder capital, contracting income, and early revenue. Most surviving
> indie studios. Slowest growth, maximum control, no dilution. The underrated option, and
> the one that keeps the definition of success yours.
> **Friends and family.** Typically ten to a hundred thousand. Use SAFEs or convertible
> notes rather than informal arrangements, and be deliberate, because a botched round here
> damages both the company and relationships that outlast it. Make sure they can afford to
> lose it and say so explicitly in writing.
> **Angels.** Individuals writing twenty-five to two hundred and fifty thousand, often
> former founders, bringing expertise and network alongside money. Found through
> introductions, communities, and demo days rather than cold outreach.
> **Venture capital.** Institutional money expecting a return profile that requires a very
> large outcome. It commits you to attempting that scale, with a board and a liquidation
> preference. Appropriate for a genuine startup by section 1's definition and actively
> harmful otherwise.
> **Publisher deals.** Per-project funding in exchange for a revenue share and often
> rights. The studio model rather than the startup model, and often the best fit for a
> single-game team. Read the rights reversion terms carefully; that clause is where the
> long-term value sits.
> **Crowdfunding.** Money plus, more importantly, proof of demand and an audience. It is a
> marketing campaign that happens to raise money, and treating it as only the latter is
> why most campaigns fail.
> **Grants.** Regional and platform funds, non-dilutive, underused because they are
> administratively tedious. Free money is worth the paperwork.

**Ask yourself:**
* What is this money actually costing me beyond the equity?
* Am I raising because I need capital, or because raising feels like progress?

## 6. Before you raise

Getting to the point where the conversation is worth having.

**Focus:** What counts as traction? Why does the story matter as much as the numbers?

**Learn:**
* [Paul Graham on fundraising](http://www.paulgraham.com/fr.html).
* Successful pitch decks published by founders, read for structure rather than content.

**Practice:** Write your traction, story, and team into three slides. Show them to someone
who has raised money and ask which of the three is weakest. It will usually be traction.

**Self-check:** you can state your traction in numbers and tell the story in under two
minutes.

> [!question]- Answer
> **Traction.** Evidence the thing is working. For software: revenue, retention, growth
> rate. For games: wishlists, demo conversion, community size, press coverage, signed
> letters of intent. Traction is the only argument that does not get dismissed, because
> everything else is a claim about the future and traction is a fact about the present.
> **Story.** Why this team, why this problem, why now. Investors fund narratives as much
> as numbers, particularly early when there are few numbers. The "why now" is the part
> founders skip and the part investors weigh most, because it asks what changed to make
> this possible or necessary today.
> **Team.** Why you specifically. Solo founders need an unusually strong advisor bench
> here, because the obvious objection is single point of failure and the answer has to be
> structural rather than reassuring.
> **The order of difficulty.** Story can be written in a week. Team can be improved in a
> quarter by recruiting advisors. Traction takes as long as it takes. If traction is weak,
> the answer is usually to wait and build rather than to pitch harder.
> **Warm introductions.** Cold outreach converts very poorly. Investors weight
> introductions from people they trust heavily, which makes network-building part of
> fundraising preparation rather than separate from it.

**Ask yourself:**
* Can I state my traction as a number that someone else would find impressive?
* What is my "why now", and is it real?

## 7. Instruments and process

What you are actually signing.

**Focus:** How does a SAFE work? What is the difference between a cap and a discount?

**Learn:**
* The [YC SAFE](https://www.ycombinator.com/documents) documents themselves. They are short.
* [Venture Deals](https://www.venturedeals.com/) by Feld and Mendelson.

**Practice:** Read a post-money SAFE line by line. Model what happens to your ownership if
you raise on a five million cap and later price a round at fifteen million.

**Self-check:** you can read a SAFE and explain caps, discounts, and pro rata rights.

> [!question]- Answer
> **SAFE.** Simple Agreement for Future Equity, from Y Combinator, now the dominant
> early-stage instrument. Money now, shares later when a priced round happens. No
> interest, no maturity date, and much less negotiation than a priced round.
> **Cap and discount.** The valuation cap sets the maximum valuation at which the SAFE
> converts, so the investor gets at least that price regardless of how high the round
> prices. The discount gives a percentage off the round price. Where both exist the
> investor takes whichever is better for them.
> **Pre-money against post-money SAFEs.** The post-money version, now standard, fixes the
> investor's percentage of the company. It is clearer for investors and dilutes founders
> more than the pre-money version did, particularly when several SAFEs stack. Founders who
> raise multiple SAFEs without modelling the aggregate are regularly surprised at
> conversion.
> **Convertible notes.** The older instrument: a loan with interest and a maturity date
> that converts to equity. More founder obligations than a SAFE and largely displaced by
> it, though still used.
> **Priced rounds.** An actual equity sale at an agreed valuation, with preferred stock
> carrying a liquidation preference and usually board rights. More expensive and slower,
> and it is where the terms that really matter appear.
> **The thing to watch.** Liquidation preference determines who gets paid first in an
> exit. A one-times non-participating preference is standard; anything more aggressive can
> mean founders receive very little from an outcome that looks like a success.

**Ask yourself:**
* If I stack three SAFEs, what do I own after conversion?
* Do I understand the liquidation preference in any term sheet I would sign?

## 8. Working with contractors

Building without hiring, without losing the company.

**Focus:** What has to be in a contractor agreement? What happens if IP assignment is
missing?

**Learn:**
* Standard contractor agreement templates, with a lawyer review once.
* Your jurisdiction's rules on contractor against employee classification.

**Practice:** Get your contractor agreement reviewed by a lawyer once. Use it every time
afterwards, including for friends, especially for friends.

**Self-check:** you can specify a contractor agreement that protects your IP, and you know
the classification risk.

> [!question]- Answer
> **IP assignment, always.** Every contractor signs one, with no exceptions for friends or
> small jobs. Without assignment, the contractor may own what they made, which surfaces
> during acquisition diligence when it is maximally expensive and least fixable.
> **What they keep.** Pre-existing IP such as their own libraries, tools, and prior work
> typically remains theirs, usually with a licence granted to you for what they used. New
> work created for the contract transfers to the company. Being explicit protects both
> sides and prevents the argument later.
> **Open source.** If contractors use or contribute to open source, the agreement should
> address licence compliance. A copyleft dependency discovered during diligence is a real
> problem.
> **Classification.** Treating someone as a contractor while directing their work like an
> employee creates real legal and tax exposure, and the tests vary by jurisdiction. The
> penalties fall on you rather than on them.
> **The friends problem.** The agreements people skip are with friends, and those are
> exactly the relationships that suffer when something is ambiguous three years later. A
> contract with a friend is a kindness to the friendship.
> **Diligence.** Every one of these surfaces during an acquisition or a funding round.
> Clean paperwork from the start costs hours; reconstructing it later costs months and
> sometimes the deal.

**Ask yourself:**
* Does anyone hold rights to something in my product that I cannot prove I own?
* Would my paperwork survive an acquirer's lawyer reading it?

## 9. Growth, moats, and exits

The long end.

**Focus:** What actually defends a business? What do the exit paths require?

**Learn:**
* [a16z on network effects](https://a16z.com/): the standard treatment of defensibility.
* *Venture Deals* again for acquisition mechanics.

**Practice:** Write down what defends your business if a well-funded competitor copies it
next year. If the answer is "we execute better", that is not a moat, and it is worth
knowing now.

**Self-check:** you can name your defensibility honestly and describe the exit paths and
what each requires.

> [!question]- Answer
> **What defends a business.** Network effects, where the product improves as more people
> use it. Switching costs. Proprietary data that compounds. Brand, which is real and slow.
> Economies of scale. Regulatory position. Execution speed is not a moat, because it is
> not durable and cannot be pointed at.
> **Games specifically.** IP and community are the durable assets. A single premium game
> has almost no moat, which is precisely why the studio model with per-project funding
> fits better than the venture model for most game makers.
> **Acquisition.** The most common positive exit. Acqui-hire buys the team and shelves the
> product. Product acquisition buys the thing. Strategic acquisition buys a market
> position and pays best. What each requires differs enormously, and clean cap tables and
> IP ownership matter in all of them.
> **IPO.** Rare, and realistically reserved for companies with very large revenue, growth,
> and clean financials. Not a plan.
> **Secondary sale.** Selling some founder shares in a later round, taking cash off the
> table without a full exit. Common from Series C onward and worth knowing exists, because
> founders otherwise hold an illiquid position for a decade.
> **The unglamorous option.** Running a profitable business indefinitely and paying
> yourself is a legitimate outcome that startup writing barely discusses, because the
> people writing it are funded by exits. If section 1 said small business, this is the
> plan rather than a fallback.

**Ask yourself:**
* What defends this if someone with more money copies it?
* Am I building toward an exit because I want one, or because the frame assumes it?

## Capstone

A written strategy document: which of the three models you are building and why, your
entity and cap table, your funding path for this project and the reasoning, your traction
plan with numbers, and your honest assessment of defensibility.

Then have someone who has actually raised money read it and tell you which part is
wishful.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish small business, startup, and studio, and say which I am building.
- [ ] I can apply the lean frameworks and name where they fit my product badly.
- [ ] I made the co-founder decision deliberately and have support structure in place.
- [ ] My entity matches my funding intent and my founder shares vest.
- [ ] I can map every funding source available to me and what each costs.
- [ ] I can state my traction in numbers and tell the story in two minutes.
- [ ] I can read a SAFE and model dilution across rounds.
- [ ] Every contractor has signed IP assignment and my paperwork would survive diligence.
- [ ] I can name my defensibility honestly, including when the answer is "none".
